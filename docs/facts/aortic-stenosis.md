# Fact sheet: Aortic stenosis

Source: Patel H, Lewis BW, Paulus R. Aortic Stenosis: Diagnosis and Treatment.
Am Fam Physician. 2026;114(2):148-155. Uploaded as PDF, 2026-09-13.

Status: draft for review. Nothing here has become a question yet.

How to read this: each fact is one standalone sentence taken from the article,
grouped into topics that could each become a planet. Tags in brackets. A star
marks facts that drive a decision at the point of care, which is where most
questions should come from. The unstarred facts are context and make good
distractors or explanations.

Five topics came out of the article. The article's pathophysiology section
was folded into "Recognising it", because its useful content is the
mechanism behind the three classic symptoms rather than the biology for its
own sake.

## Topic A. Who gets aortic stenosis (14 facts)

Frame: the patient in front of you, before any murmur. Risk, prevalence,
natural history.
Tags: epidemiology, risk-factors, bicuspid, natural-history, prognosis

1. Aortic stenosis is the most common valvular heart disease in the United
   States, affecting up to 3.7% of the general population. [epidemiology]
2. Prevalence climbs steeply with age: 0.2% in the 50s, 1.3% in the 60s,
   3.9% in the 70s, 9.8% in the 80s. [epidemiology, age]
3. ★ Untreated symptomatic severe aortic stenosis carries an estimated
   two-year mortality of 50%. [prognosis, natural-history]
4. The disease process resembles atherosclerosis: LDL and lipoprotein(a)
   accumulate on the valve and trigger inflammation and calcification.
   [pathophysiology]
5. ★ Aortic sclerosis is calcification without restricted leaflet mobility;
   about 2% of people with sclerosis progress to stenosis each year.
   [natural-history, sclerosis]
6. Nonmodifiable risk factors: age over 65, bicuspid aortic valve, male sex,
   elevated lipoprotein(a). [risk-factors]
7. Modifiable risk factors: coronary artery disease, hypertension, diabetes,
   dyslipidemia, tobacco use, impaired kidney function, thoracic radiation,
   rheumatic disease. [risk-factors]
8. ★ Bicuspid aortic valve is present in 1% to 2% of the US population, and
   more than half of those people eventually need valve replacement.
   [bicuspid]
9. Bicuspid valves can calcify in a patient's 40s and need replacement at a
   younger age than tricuspid valves. [bicuspid, age]
10. Elevated lipoprotein(a) raises the odds of aortic stenosis (odds ratio
    1.2) in genome-wide meta-analysis. [lipoprotein-a, risk-factors]
11. Severe stenosis is more likely with hypertension (hazard ratio 1.71),
    diabetes (1.49) and dyslipidemia (1.17). [risk-factors]
12. Most patients are asymptomatic at first, through a long latent period,
    and develop symptoms only as stenosis becomes severe. [natural-history]
13. Prevalence has risen over recent decades and is projected to keep rising
    for the next 25 years. [epidemiology]
14. Wearable-device data associate higher moderate-to-vigorous activity with
    lower rates of aortic stenosis and intervention, but the finding is
    observational and needs confirmation. [prevention, activity]

## Topic B. Recognising it (16 facts)

Frame: history and examination, and why symptoms get missed.
Tags: symptoms, atypical, examination, likelihood-ratio, atrial-fibrillation,
heyde, pathophysiology

1. ★ Classic symptoms: exertional angina, dyspnea, presyncope, syncope, and
   symptoms of heart failure. [symptoms]
2. ★ Early or atypical symptoms are fatigue, exercise intolerance and
   lightheadedness, and are often attributed to aging or another disease.
   [symptoms, atypical]
3. A careful history is critical because early symptoms are subtle
   (SORT C, expert consensus). [history, sort]
4. New aortic stenosis is sometimes found during an acute exacerbation of a
   comorbid condition such as atrial fibrillation or decompensated heart
   failure. [presentation]
5. ★ About 16% of patients with aortic stenosis have concurrent atrial
   fibrillation, and that group had a lower rate of valve replacement at one
   year (hazard ratio 0.73), because symptoms get attributed to the
   arrhythmia. [atrial-fibrillation]
6. Coronary artery disease coexists in 24% to 68% of patients; telling the
   two apart is hard, so a multidisciplinary approach is recommended. [cad]
7. ★ Heyde syndrome is aortic stenosis with gastrointestinal bleeding and
   acquired von Willebrand disease; valve replacement improves the
   coagulopathy. [heyde]
8. Think of Heyde syndrome in a patient with gastrointestinal bleeding and a
   murmur. [heyde, presentation]
9. ★ The characteristic murmur is loud, late-peaking and systolic, best heard
   at the right upper sternal border, radiating to the carotids.
   [examination]
10. Other findings: an absent or diminished second heart sound, and a
    dampened, delayed carotid upstroke (pulsus parvus et tardus).
    [examination]
11. ★ The findings that most raise the likelihood of aortic stenosis are an
    inaudible second heart sound (LR+ 15.7), a murmur radiating to both
    carotids (LR+ 12) and a delayed carotid upstroke (LR+ 6.3).
    [examination, likelihood-ratio]
12. ★ Auscultation alone is unreliable: against echocardiography it has 44%
    sensitivity and 69% specificity for significant valvular disease.
    [examination, accuracy]
13. The absence of a murmur has a negative predictive value of 88% for
    significant valvular disease. [examination, accuracy]
14. Angina can occur without coronary disease, from reduced coronary flow
    reserve and myocardial perfusion in the hypertrophied ventricle.
    [pathophysiology, angina]
15. Syncope comes from fixed outflow obstruction and falling cardiac output
    dropping blood pressure. [pathophysiology, syncope]
16. The narrowed valve raises left ventricular pressure, causing concentric
    hypertrophy and diastolic dysfunction; when the ventricle can no longer
    compensate, output falls and systolic dysfunction and heart failure
    follow. [pathophysiology]

## Topic C. Confirming and grading it (15 facts)

Frame: the echo report and what the numbers mean.
Tags: echo, thresholds, severity, low-flow, staging, screening

1. ★ Transthoracic echocardiography is the preferred diagnostic test: it is
   noninvasive, relatively affordable, gives anatomic and functional
   information, and classifies severity. [echo]
2. ★ Echocardiography is indicated for an unexplained systolic murmur, or for
   symptoms plus examination findings suggestive of aortic stenosis
   (SORT C). [echo, indication, sort]
3. There is no screening recommendation, though modelling suggests a
   one-time screen at age 80 may improve quality of life and cost.
   [screening]
4. Severity is graded by three echo measures: maximum transaortic velocity
   (Vmax), mean pressure gradient, and aortic valve area. [echo, severity]
5. Mild: valve area over 1.5 cm², Vmax 2.0 to 2.9 m/s, mean gradient under
   20 mm Hg. [thresholds]
6. Moderate: valve area 1.0 to 1.5 cm², Vmax 3.0 to 3.9 m/s, mean gradient
   20 to 39 mm Hg. [thresholds]
7. ★ Severe: valve area under 1 cm², Vmax over 4 m/s, mean gradient 40 mm Hg
   or more. [thresholds]
8. Patients are often asymptomatic until Vmax exceeds 4 m/s.
   [natural-history, thresholds]
9. ★ Low-flow, low-gradient severe stenosis: valve area under 1 cm² but Vmax
   under 4 m/s and gradient under 40 mm Hg, because a reduced stroke volume
   underestimates both. It is still classified as severe. [low-flow]
10. If the cause of symptoms is unclear after echo, coronary artery calcium
    scoring on CT or exercise stress testing can help decide whether to
    intervene. [workup]
11. Stage A is "at risk": no stenosis, possibly sclerosis; management is
    risk factor modification. [staging]
12. ★ Stage B is progressive, asymptomatic mild or moderate stenosis: repeat
    echo every 3 to 5 years if mild, every 1 to 2 years if moderate.
    [staging, monitoring]
13. ★ Stage C is asymptomatic severe stenosis: refer to cardiology; replace
    the valve if ejection fraction is under 50%, if other cardiac surgery is
    planned, if Vmax is over 5 m/s, if a stress test is positive, or if Vmax
    rises more than 0.3 m/s in a year; otherwise repeat echo every 6 to 12
    months. [staging, indication]
14. ★ Stage D is symptomatic severe stenosis: prompt referral for valve
    replacement. [staging, indication]
15. The ACC/AHA guideline stages by severity plus symptoms, and dedicated
    heart teams (interventional cardiology, cardiothoracic surgery,
    anesthesia) decide whether and when to replace the valve. [heart-team]

## Topic D. Living with it (12 facts)

Frame: what the family physician does between diagnosis and referral.
Tags: management, statin, hypertension, activity, monitoring, referral

1. ★ No treatment reverses aortic stenosis or prevents the need for valve
   replacement. [management]
2. The focus of care is optimising comorbid conditions to reduce
   cardiovascular mortality. [management]
3. ★ Treating hypertension and dyslipidemia does not change the course of the
   stenosis, but is recommended to lower atherosclerotic risk (SORT B).
   [management, sort]
4. ★ Atorvastatin 80 mg daily did not slow calcification or stenosis in a
   randomised trial; statins are still recommended according to the
   patient's ten-year cardiovascular risk. [statin]
5. Bisphosphonates do not affect progression of valve calcification
   (randomised trial). [bisphosphonate]
6. ★ Uncontrolled blood pressure raises mortality; no antihypertensive is
   superior, and treatment should start low and titrate slowly because
   excessive vasodilation can cause presyncope or syncope. [hypertension]
7. After transcatheter valve replacement, ACE inhibitors and ARBs were
   associated with lower mortality (relative risk 0.74) and less new-onset
   atrial fibrillation (0.71). [acei-arb, post-tavr]
8. ★ Activity: no limits with mild stenosis; low to moderate intensity with
   moderate; low intensity only with severe. [activity]
9. ★ Asymptomatic patients get serial echo every 3 to 5 years for mild, 1 to
   2 years for moderate, 6 to 12 months for severe. [monitoring]
10. ★ Clinical triggers for an earlier repeat echo: new symptoms, a louder
    murmur, a later-peaking murmur, or a newly absent second heart sound.
    [monitoring]
11. ★ Refer to cardiology for severe stenosis, and consider referral for
    moderate. [referral]
12. Once stenosis is identified, the family physician should stage it to set
    the monitoring interval and the timing of referral. [role]

## Topic E. Replacing the valve (18 facts)

Frame: indications, choosing the route, and the care that follows.
Tags: indication, stress-test, tavr, savr, anticoagulation, inr, prophylaxis

1. ★ The primary indication is symptomatic severe stenosis, which needs
   prompt cardiology evaluation because valve replacement is the only
   treatment that improves mortality (SORT B). [indication, sort]
2. ★ Asymptomatic severe stenosis should also go to replacement when ejection
   fraction is under 50%, Vmax is over 5 m/s, or a stress test is positive.
   [indication]
3. ★ A supervised exercise stress test can unmask symptoms in apparently
   asymptomatic severe stenosis, but must be avoided in patients who already
   have symptoms. [stress-test]
4. A positive test means symptoms of stenosis or a fall in systolic blood
   pressure of at least 10 mm Hg, and warrants replacement. [stress-test]
5. Patients having other cardiac surgery who also have moderate or severe
   stenosis should have the valve replaced at the same time. [indication]
6. Trials of early intervention versus watchful waiting in asymptomatic
   severe stenosis have mixed results. [evidence]
7. The two routes are transcatheter replacement (TAVR) and open surgical
   replacement. [tavr, savr]
8. TAVR uses bioprosthetic valves; surgery can use bioprosthetic or
   mechanical valves. [tavr, savr, valve-type]
9. Route and valve type depend on age, preference, life expectancy, and
   ability to tolerate anticoagulation. [choice]
10. ★ Randomised trials and meta-analyses show no difference in short- or
    long-term mortality between TAVR and surgery, regardless of surgical
    risk. [outcomes]
11. Outcomes are similar for bicuspid valves. [outcomes, bicuspid]
12. ★ TAVR carries more vascular complications, pacemaker need and
    reintervention; surgery carries more new-onset atrial fibrillation and
    severe bleeding. [complications]
13. ★ Because long-term TAVR durability is unproven, surgery is first-line at
    65 or younger, or with a life expectancy over 20 years. [choice]
14. ★ A mechanical valve needs lifelong anticoagulation with a vitamin K
    antagonist such as warfarin. [anticoagulation]
15. A surgical bioprosthetic valve needs a vitamin K antagonist for 3 to 6
    months, then lifelong aspirin 75 to 100 mg. [anticoagulation]
16. After TAVR: a vitamin K antagonist or dual antiplatelet therapy for 3 to
    6 months, then lifelong aspirin. [anticoagulation]
17. ★ INR goal with a mechanical valve is 2.0 to 3.0, rising to 2.5 to 3.5 with
    atrial fibrillation, prior venous thromboembolism, a known hypercoagulable
    state, or left ventricular systolic dysfunction. [inr]
18. ★ After valve replacement, dental procedures need endocarditis
    prophylaxis: amoxicillin 2 g as a single dose 30 to 60 minutes before.
    [prophylaxis]

## Notes for question writing

- Topics C and E are the strongest for decision questions and the natural
  homes for multiple-answer questions (indications for replacement, INR risk
  factors, triggers for repeat echo, complications by route).
- Topic A is the weakest for decisions. Its best questions are natural
  history (sclerosis progression, bicuspid outlook, two-year mortality).
- Topics B and D share the confounders theme (atrial fibrillation, coronary
  disease); write those questions once and keep them in B.
- The thresholds in Topic C facts 5 to 7 are one fact each but support
  several question shapes: grade this echo, which number makes it severe,
  which patient is low-flow low-gradient.
